Strong Passwords and Password Managers: PNG Beginner Guide

Audience: Everyone — home users, parents, SME staff, young people
Difficulty: Beginner
Time: 20 minutes
Last reviewed: June 2026

1 Quick Summary

Creating strong, unique passwords and using a password manager are the most effective ways to protect your online accounts. This guide explains what makes a password strong, how to create one you can remember, and how to use a free password manager to handle passwords you cannot remember.

You do not need to be technical to follow this guide. Each step is written for someone setting up password security for the first time.

What you will achieve: After completing this guide, your most important accounts will have strong, unique passwords. You will understand how to create passphrases, and you will have a method for storing passwords safely — either using a password manager app or a secure physical record.

2 Before You Start

What you need

  • Your phone or computer with internet access
  • Access to your email, Facebook, and banking accounts (you will be updating passwords)
  • A notebook or secure place to record passwords if you are not using a password manager app

Priority accounts to secure first

Not all accounts carry the same risk. Start with the highest-value targets:

  1. Email account — your email is used to reset all other account passwords. If someone accesses your email, they can take over everything else.
  2. Facebook — used for communication, business, and sometimes payments in PNG
  3. Banking and mobile money apps — BSP Mobile, Kina Bank, Westly, etc.
  4. Work or business systems — accounting software, business email, CRM

3 PNG Reality Check

PNG Reality Check

Many PNG users use the same simple password — such as their first name, birthday, or phone number — across multiple accounts including Facebook, banking apps, and email. When one account is compromised, all accounts with the same password become vulnerable immediately.

Mobile-first users in PNG typically manage 5–10 accounts on a single phone. If that phone is lost, stolen, or accessed by someone else, and all accounts share the same password, every account is at risk.

The most common weak passwords seen in PNG and the Pacific region include: first name + birth year (e.g. "Maria1990"), phone number, "password", "123456", "png2024", and combinations of family members' names. These are among the first passwords attackers try. ASSUMED — based on regional cybersecurity patterns

4 Safety Warning

Never Share Your Passwords

Never share your passwords with anyone — including people who claim to be from Facebook, your bank, Digicel, or a technical support team. A real support agent will never ask for your password. They do not need it to help you.

If you receive a call, SMS, or WhatsApp message from someone asking for your password, or telling you to read out a code that was just sent to your phone — stop immediately. This is a scam. Hang up or do not reply.

If you believe you have already shared your password with someone, change it immediately using the steps in this guide.

5 Step-by-Step Instructions

Step 1

Understand What Makes a Password Strong

A strong password has several characteristics that make it hard to guess or crack. Understanding these helps you create good passwords for every account.

A strong password:

  • Is at least 12 characters long (longer is better)
  • Mixes uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols (e.g. !, @, #, -, _)
  • Is unique — not used for any other account
  • Is not predictable based on personal information

A weak password:

  • Contains your name, birthday, phone number, or address
  • Is a common word or phrase: "password", "letmein", "admin", "qwerty"
  • Uses simple patterns: "123456", "abc123", "111111"
  • Is the same as, or very similar to, a password used on another account
  • Is short — under 8 characters
Length is the most important factor. A 16-character password made of random words is stronger than an 8-character password full of symbols.
Step 2

Create a Strong Passphrase

A passphrase is a password made from multiple random words joined together. It is easier to remember than a string of random characters, but just as strong — or stronger — because of its length.

How to create a passphrase

  1. Choose 3 or 4 completely random, unrelated words. Pick words that are not related to you personally. Example: River, Smoke, Table, Mountain — but choose your own, different words.
  2. Add a number and a symbol somewhere in the phrase.
  3. Combine them with hyphens, spaces, or other separators.
Example passphrase pattern (do not use this exact example): "River-Smoke-Table-47!" — This pattern demonstrates structure only. Create your own with different, random words.

A passphrase like this is 22+ characters and takes billions of years to crack by brute force. It is also possible to remember without writing it down — but writing it down in a secure location is still recommended.

Step 3

Use a Unique Password for Every Account

Using the same password for multiple accounts is the single most common security mistake. If one site has a data breach and your password is leaked, attackers will immediately try it on your email, Facebook, and banking accounts.

  1. Identify your 4–5 most important accounts (email, Facebook, banking, work).
  2. Check: do any of them share a password? If yes, change them now.
  3. Create a separate, unique password for each important account.
  4. Less critical accounts (forums, shopping sites) still benefit from unique passwords — a password manager makes this easy.
If you find this difficult to manage, this is exactly the problem a password manager solves — see Step 4.
Step 4

Consider Using a Free Password Manager

A password manager is an app that securely stores all your passwords behind one master password. You only need to remember one strong password; the manager remembers the rest.

What a password manager does

  • Stores your passwords in encrypted form on your device or in the cloud
  • Generates strong, unique passwords automatically when you create new accounts
  • Fills in passwords automatically when you log in to websites or apps
  • Alerts you if a saved password appears in a known data breach

Free password manager options

  • Bitwarden — Free, open-source, available for Android and iOS. The free tier has no limits on the number of passwords stored. Recommended for PNG users who want a dedicated, independent password manager. ASSUMED available in PNG via Google Play and App Store — verify
  • Google Password Manager — Built into Chrome browser and Android phones. If you already use a Google/Gmail account, this is already available to you at no cost. Passwords sync across devices logged into the same Google account.
  • Apple iCloud Keychain — Built into iPhones and iPads. Works automatically if you use Safari and an Apple account. Passwords sync across Apple devices.

Getting started with Bitwarden (recommended for new users)

  1. Go to bitwarden.com or search "Bitwarden" in the Google Play Store or Apple App Store.
  2. Create a free account using your email address.
  3. Set a strong master password — this is the one password you must remember and never forget. Use a passphrase (see Step 2). Write it down and store it very safely.
  4. Add your existing account passwords to Bitwarden, one at a time.
  5. From now on, when you create a new account anywhere, use Bitwarden's password generator to create a strong, unique password and save it immediately.
If you prefer not to use an app: A secure written record is acceptable for a small number of accounts. Use a notebook kept in a safe location at home — not a notebook kept in your bag or work desk. Write clearly, and do not label it obviously as "passwords".
Step 5

Set Up Recovery Options on Critical Accounts

Account recovery options help you regain access if you forget your password or lose your phone. Setting these up now prevents a crisis later.

  1. For Gmail/Google: Go to myaccount.google.com → Security → Ways we can verify it's you. Add a recovery phone number and a recovery email address.
  2. For Facebook: Settings & Privacy → Settings → Security and Login → Recovery Codes. Generate and save recovery codes. Also add a trusted contact or recovery email.
  3. For banking apps: Contact your bank directly. Most banks link recovery to your registered mobile number — ensure your bank has your current number.
  4. Print or write down recovery codes for your most critical accounts. Store them in a secure physical location — not in a phone notes app unless that app is itself password-protected.
Recovery codes are single-use backup codes that allow you to log in if you cannot access your usual 2FA method. Store them as carefully as you would store cash.

6 Recommended Settings

Setting Recommendation Notes
Minimum password length 12 characters or more Longer is always better. 16+ characters is preferred for high-value accounts.
Character types Uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols Mixing character types increases strength. For passphrases, include at least one number and one symbol.
Password reuse Never reuse passwords across accounts Each account must have its own unique password. A password manager makes this practical.
Storage method Password manager app or secure written record Do not store passwords in SMS messages, WhatsApp, or unprotected phone notes.
Password manager master password Passphrase — 4 random words + number + symbol This must be memorised and also written down in a secure physical location.

7 Common Mistakes

Mistake Why It Matters Fix
Using your name, birthday, or phone number as a password This information is often publicly visible on your Facebook profile. Attackers check this information first. Change to a passphrase with no connection to your personal details.
Using the same password for multiple accounts One breach exposes all accounts. Many large websites have suffered data breaches where passwords were leaked. Use a unique password for every account. Use a password manager to manage them.
Saving passwords in SMS or WhatsApp messages If your phone or account is accessed by someone else, all saved passwords are exposed. Use a password manager or a written notebook stored securely at home.
Writing passwords on a visible sticky note near your computer or phone Anyone who sees the note — visitors, colleagues, family members — has access to your accounts. Store written passwords in a locked or secure location. Do not display them.
Sharing passwords with family members or colleagues "temporarily" Once shared, you cannot control who else that person shares it with. Temporary often becomes permanent. Use account sharing features where available (e.g. Netflix Family, business user accounts). Change passwords after any temporary sharing.
Choosing passwords based on patterns (abc123, Password1!) Attackers use dictionary attacks and pattern-matching. These are among the first guesses tried. Use random words or a password manager's generator — not patterns.

8 Troubleshooting

Problem Likely Cause What to Try
Forgot password and cannot log in Password changed and not recorded Use the "Forgot Password" or "Reset Password" link on the login page. You will need access to your recovery email or phone number.
Forgot master password for password manager Master password not written down Bitwarden and most password managers cannot reset the master password — this is a security feature. If recovery codes were set up, use those. Otherwise you may need to reset the account (you will lose stored passwords).
Password manager app not available in PNG Play Store / App Store Regional availability restrictions Bitwarden's web vault is accessible at vault.bitwarden.com from any browser. Google Password Manager works without a separate app install if you use Chrome.
Website says password is too short or requires certain characters Website has specific password requirements Use the password manager's generator with the site's requirements set — or add a symbol and number to your passphrase to meet the requirements.

9 When to Call for Help

Password security is generally something you can manage yourself. Seek help if:

  • You believe your email or banking account has already been accessed by someone else — contact your bank immediately and change all linked account passwords
  • You are setting up password security for a business with multiple staff — consider a business password manager (e.g. Bitwarden Teams) rather than individual accounts
  • Your organisation stores highly sensitive data and needs a formal password policy — consult a cybersecurity professional
  • You are locked out of a critical account and recovery options are not working — contact the platform's official support
