How to Safely Use Public Wi-Fi and Internet Cafés

Audience: Students, travellers, anyone using internet cafés or public Wi-Fi in PNG
Difficulty: Beginner
Time: 15 minutes
Last reviewed: June 2026

1 Quick Summary

Public Wi-Fi and internet cafés are widely used across PNG for job applications, government services, online banking, Facebook, and email. They provide affordable and convenient internet access — but using them without taking precautions can expose your accounts and personal information to serious risk.

This guide explains the specific risks of public and shared internet access, and gives you practical steps to protect yourself whenever you use a shared computer or public Wi-Fi connection.

The most important rules: Never use internet banking on a shared computer. Always log out of every account before leaving. Never save passwords to a shared browser. Use private/incognito mode whenever you access personal accounts on a shared device.

2 PNG Reality Check

PNG Reality Check

Internet cafés and public Wi-Fi spots in PNG are commonly used for job applications, government services, banking, Facebook, and email access. For many users, especially students and people without home internet, a café is their primary way of accessing the internet. ASSUMED — general PNG internet café use pattern; confirm with local usage data

Shared computers at internet cafés may have been used by dozens of people before you. If the café does not regularly clean and reset computers, software like keyloggers — programs that record every key you type, including passwords — may have been installed by a previous user. Session data, saved passwords, and browser history from previous users may also be accessible.

Public Wi-Fi networks at cafés, hotels, or public spaces may not encrypt traffic between your device and the router. This means other users on the same network may be able to intercept some of your data, particularly if you visit websites without HTTPS.

3 Critical Safety Warning

Banking and Shared Computers — Important

Never access your internet banking, mobile money account, or any financial service on a shared computer at an internet café. A keylogger on that computer will capture your login credentials, PIN, and any amounts you transfer — without you seeing anything unusual happen on screen.

If you need to check your bank balance or transfer money while away from home, use your own phone with your own mobile data connection instead of the café's Wi-Fi or shared computer.

Always log out of all accounts — Facebook, email, government portals, and any other service — before leaving a shared device. Simply closing the browser tab is not enough. The next person to use that computer may be able to reopen your session.

4 Understanding the Risks

Knowing what can go wrong helps you make better decisions about what to do on a public or shared connection and what to avoid.

Risk What It Means How Common
Keyloggers Software secretly installed on the shared computer that records every keystroke you make — including usernames, passwords, and messages. You will not see any sign it is running. Risk present in unmanaged café environments — confirm local data
Session hijacking If you do not log out properly, the next person to use the computer may be able to click "back" or reopen the browser and find themselves already logged into your account. Very common with Facebook and email when users close the tab without logging out
Saved passwords If the browser asks "Save your password?" and a previous user clicked Yes, their login details may auto-fill on shared sites — or yours may be saved for the next user. Common — most browsers default to offering to save passwords
Unsecured Wi-Fi traffic On some public Wi-Fi networks, other users on the same network can see what websites you visit and, on HTTP (not HTTPS) sites, may be able to read the data you send. Reduced by HTTPS adoption on most modern websites, but still relevant for older sites
Fake hotspots An attacker sets up a Wi-Fi hotspot with a convincing name (e.g. "CaféFreeWifi" near a café that offers free Wi-Fi) to intercept traffic from people who connect to it instead of the real network. Risk pattern confirmed globally — local PNG prevalence unknown

5 Steps for Safe Use

Step 1

Verify the Wi-Fi Network Name Before Connecting

Before connecting to any Wi-Fi at a café, hotel, or public space, ask a staff member for the exact network name (SSID). Do not assume the strongest or most obvious Wi-Fi signal belongs to the establishment.

  • Ask staff: "What is the name of your Wi-Fi network?"
  • Compare the name they give you with the networks listed on your phone.
  • If you see multiple networks with similar names (e.g. "CaféWiFi" and "CaféWiFi_Free"), ask staff which one is theirs.
  • Avoid connecting to networks with generic names like "Free WiFi", "Open Network", or "Customer Access" if you cannot verify them with staff.
You should be able to confirm the network name directly with a staff member. If no staff member is available to confirm, treat any Wi-Fi network at that location as untrusted.
Step 2

Check That Websites Use HTTPS

HTTPS (the padlock icon in your browser address bar) means the connection between your browser and the website is encrypted. On an HTTPS site, other people on the same Wi-Fi network cannot easily read the specific content you are viewing or the data you submit.

  • Look for a padlock icon to the left of the website address in the browser address bar.
  • Most modern websites — Google, Facebook, banking sites, government portals — use HTTPS by default.
  • If a site shows "Not Secure" or no padlock, do not enter any login credentials or personal information.
The padlock icon should be visible in the browser address bar before you type a password or personal details into any website on a public network.
Step 3

Avoid Sensitive Tasks on Public Networks

Some tasks should not be done on public Wi-Fi or shared computers regardless of other precautions:

  • Do not access internet banking or mobile money on a shared computer — use your own phone with mobile data.
  • Avoid accessing work email with sensitive business information on an unmanaged shared computer.
  • Do not submit payroll information, contract details, or confidential documents from a shared computer.
  • For genuinely sensitive tasks, use your own personal device with your own mobile data connection.
If you are unsure whether a task is sensitive enough to avoid on a shared computer, ask yourself: "Would it matter if someone else saw this?" If yes, use your own device and data.
Step 4

Before You Start on a Shared Computer

When you sit down at a shared computer, take these steps before logging into anything:

  1. Check whether the browser is already logged into someone else's account. If you see another person's name or account, close that browser session without accessing their accounts, and open a new private/incognito window.
  2. Open a private or incognito browser window: press Ctrl + Shift + N in Chrome or Edge, or Ctrl + Shift + P in Firefox. A private window does not save your history, cookies, or passwords after you close it.
  3. Do all your browsing within this private window.
The browser window should show an icon or label indicating it is in private or incognito mode (often a dark theme or a hat-and-glasses icon).
Step 5

When You Are Finished — Log Out Properly

Before you leave a shared computer, complete each of these steps. Do not skip any of them.

  1. Log out of every account you accessed — Facebook, email, government portals, any other login. Go to the account menu and click "Log Out" or "Sign Out". Do not just close the browser tab.
  2. Clear browser history and cookies: press Ctrl + Shift + Delete in most browsers. Select "All time" as the time range and tick History, Cookies, Cached images and files, and Saved passwords. Click Clear.
  3. When prompted "Save your password?" — always click Never or No. If you accidentally clicked Yes on a previous visit, go to browser Settings > Passwords and delete any saved passwords for your accounts.
  4. Close the private browsing window.
After clearing history and logging out, reload the pages you were using to confirm you are no longer logged in. The site should show you as a guest or prompt you to log in again.
Step 6

After Using a Public Computer — Change Your Password

If you accessed Facebook, email, or other accounts from a shared computer and you are not confident the computer was secure (no visible antivirus, no regular maintenance, an unfamiliar café), change the passwords for those accounts as soon as you are back on your own device.

This is especially important if:

  • The café computer appeared to have unknown or unusual software running
  • The computer was very slow or showed pop-up ads while you were using it (possible signs of malware)
  • You used the computer for a financial service, even briefly
Password change is a precaution, not a guarantee — but it limits the window of risk if your credentials were captured.
