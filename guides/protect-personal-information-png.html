How to Protect Your Personal Information Online

Audience: Everyone
Difficulty: Beginner
Time: 20 minutes
Last reviewed: June 2026

1 Quick Summary

Personal information shared online can be used to impersonate you, access your accounts, target you with scams, or cause harm to your reputation and safety. The information you share on social media, in online forms, and on shared devices is often more accessible than you realise.

This guide explains what personal information to protect, which settings to review on your phone and Facebook account, and practical habits to reduce your exposure. These steps are practical for anyone using a smartphone or shared computer in PNG.

What you will achieve: After completing this guide, your Facebook profile will share less sensitive information publicly, your phone apps will have only the permissions they need, and you will have habits in place that reduce the amount of personal information you leave behind online.

2 Before You Start

What you need

  • Your phone, or the computer you use most often
  • Your Facebook account login (you will be reviewing privacy settings)
  • About 20 minutes to go through each step

This guide does not require any technical skills. It is about reviewing settings you already have access to — most people just have not checked them before.

3 PNG Reality Check

PNG Reality Check

In PNG, many users share personal details on their Facebook profiles — including full name, employer, home district, phone number, and family photos. When this information is set to "Public", anyone in the world can see it. Scammers, impersonators, and people with harmful intentions use this publicly available information to target specific individuals.

On shared devices — family phones, internet café computers, school computers — personal information may remain accessible to the next person who uses the device if accounts are not properly logged out and browsers are not cleared.

In PNG's social and community context, there can also be offline risks associated with publicly sharing location information, daily routines, or your home community. This guide focuses on online privacy settings, but those settings also have real-world safety implications. ASSUMED — applies broadly, specific local context requires community input

4 High-Value Personal Information

Protect These Details Most Carefully

Some personal details are more valuable to scammers and identity thieves than others. Be very careful about where and with whom you share:

  • Full date of birth (day, month, and year combined)
  • National ID or passport number
  • Tax Identification Number (TIN)
  • Bank account number and BSB/sort code
  • Mobile money account number or PIN
  • Mother's maiden name
  • Username and passwords for any account

Banks and official agencies already have these details — they will not contact you to ask you to confirm them by SMS, email, or phone. If someone asks, it is a scam.

5 What Counts as Personal Information

Personal information is any information that can identify you or be used to locate, contact, or impersonate you. This includes information that seems harmless on its own but becomes powerful when combined.

Common personal information to protect

  • Full name
  • Date of birth (even just the year)
  • Home address and community/district
  • Current location or regular locations
  • Phone number
  • Email address
  • National ID or passport number
  • Tax Identification Number (TIN)
  • Bank account and card details
  • Usernames and passwords
  • Mother's maiden name
  • Employer and workplace name and address
  • Photos of yourself, especially ones that show your face clearly
  • Photos of family members, especially children
  • Photos that reveal your home, vehicle, or regular location
  • Photos of identity documents, bank cards, or receipts
Combination risk: Your name and district might each seem harmless. But your name + employer + phone number + district + profile photo + daily routine together create a detailed profile that can be used for targeted scams, harassment, or identity fraud.

6 Step-by-Step Instructions

Step 1

Review Your Facebook Privacy Settings

Facebook has detailed privacy controls that most users never configure. The defaults often share more than you would choose to share if you knew.

  1. Open Facebook. Tap the three lines (menu) and go to Settings & Privacy → Settings → Privacy.
  2. Who can see your future posts? — Change to Friends (not Public). Posts set to Public are visible to anyone, including people you do not know.
  3. Who can see your friends list? — Change to Friends or Only Me. A public friends list allows scammers to identify your contacts for impersonation attacks.
  4. Who can look you up using your phone number? — Change to Friends or Only Me.
  5. Who can look you up using your email? — Change to Friends or Only Me.
  6. Go to your profile → Edit Profile → review each "About" section (Work, Education, Places Lived, Contact Info, Basic Info). Set sensitive fields (phone, email, birthday) to Only Me.
  7. On the Facebook app, go to Settings → Location → turn off location access or set to "While using the app" only if needed.
After changing these settings, view your own profile from the perspective of someone who is not your friend. Tap the three dots on your profile → "View As". Check what a stranger can see.
Step 2

Be Careful What You Post Publicly

Privacy settings help, but the best protection is also being thoughtful about what you post in the first place. Settings can be changed back by accident, or by a platform update — content you never posted cannot be leaked.

  • Avoid posting your home address, neighbourhood details, or travel plans publicly.
  • Avoid posting photos that show the front of your house or any security features.
  • Avoid sharing your daily routine publicly (when you leave for work, when children leave for school).
  • Do not post photos of important documents — National ID, passport, bank cards, receipts showing account numbers.
  • Be cautious about checking in to locations in real time. Checking in after you have returned from somewhere is safer than checking in while you are there, especially if you are at home.
Step 3

Check App Permissions on Your Phone

Many apps on your phone request access to location, camera, microphone, contacts, and SMS messages. Some of this access is necessary — a maps app needs location. But many apps collect data they do not need to function.

On Android:

  1. Go to Settings → Apps (or Application Manager).
  2. Tap on an app, then tap Permissions.
  3. Review each permission. Ask: does this app need this to work?
  4. A flashlight app does not need your contacts or location. A game does not need your microphone. Remove access that seems unnecessary.
  5. Alternatively, go to Settings → Privacy → Permission Manager to see all apps that have a particular permission (e.g. all apps with location access).

On iPhone/iPad:

  1. Go to Settings → Privacy & Security.
  2. Tap each category (Location Services, Contacts, Camera, Microphone, etc.) to see which apps have access.
  3. For location: set most apps to "Never" or "While Using the App". Very few apps need "Always On" location access.
  4. Remove access you did not intentionally grant or no longer want.
Pay particular attention to apps you downloaded for a one-time purpose and then stopped using. Delete apps you no longer use — they may still be collecting data in the background.
Step 4

Be Careful with Online Forms and Websites

Online forms — for competitions, job applications, services, or surveys — often ask for personal information. Before entering your details:

  1. Check that the website address begins with https:// (the padlock icon in your browser). This means the connection is encrypted. However, this does not guarantee the site is legitimate — scam sites can also use https. You still need to check the domain name is correct.
  2. Check the domain name. "bspbank.com.pg" looks different from "bsp-bank-login.com" — the second one is fake.
  3. Be cautious about online competitions and surveys that ask for detailed personal information — name, address, date of birth, phone number. These details can be sold or used for targeted scams.
  4. Only enter banking or financial details on websites you navigated to yourself by typing the address — not by following a link in an email or SMS.
Step 5

Protect Your Information on Shared Devices

If you use a shared phone, family computer, internet café computer, or school computer, your personal information can be accessed by others if you do not take precautions.

  1. Log out of all accounts before leaving or handing a device to someone else. Do not just close the browser — use the account's Log Out option.
  2. On a shared computer or internet café: open your browser in Private or Incognito mode before logging into any personal account. This prevents the browser from saving your login details, history, or cookies.
  3. After using a shared device, clear browser history and saved passwords: in most browsers, go to Settings → Privacy → Clear Browsing Data. Select history, cookies, and cached files.
  4. Never save passwords on shared devices when the browser offers to "Remember this password".
  5. Do not use shared devices for banking or mobile money transactions unless absolutely necessary. If you must, use the bank's official app if installed, or private browsing mode, and log out completely when finished.

7 Common Mistakes

Mistake Why It Matters Fix
Leaving Facebook profile set to Public Anyone in the world can see your posts, photos, employer, phone number, and location Change "Who can see your posts" to Friends. Review About section and limit sensitive fields to Only Me.
Posting photos of documents or receipts Account numbers, ID numbers, and other details in photos can be used for identity fraud Never photograph and post documents. If you must share a receipt, cover or blur identifying numbers first.
Granting all app permissions without reviewing them Apps may collect location, contacts, and other data beyond what they need Review permissions for each app. Remove access that is not needed for the app's core function.
Not logging out of accounts on shared devices The next person to use the device has access to your accounts and messages Always use the account's Log Out option on shared devices. Use private browsing mode.
Entering personal details into unknown websites Fake websites collect your information for scam or identity theft purposes Check the URL carefully. Only enter details on sites you navigated to yourself from a known, trusted source.

9 Troubleshooting

Problem What to Do
I cannot find the Privacy settings on Facebook On the Facebook app: tap the three lines menu → Settings & Privacy → Settings → Privacy. Settings locations change with app updates — search "Privacy" in the Settings search bar if needed.
I think someone has already used my personal information Check your bank account for unusual transactions. Check your Facebook and email for logins from unrecognised devices. Change passwords and enable 2FA. Contact your bank if there are financial irregularities.
An app I need asks for permissions I am not comfortable with Deny the permission and see if the app still works for your purpose. Many apps work without all requested permissions. If the app requires a permission you are not comfortable with, consider whether you need the app at all.
I used a shared computer and forgot to log out Log in to your accounts from another device immediately. Check activity logs for any unexpected access. Change your password if you are concerned.

10 When to Get Help

  • If you believe your identity has been stolen — contact your bank, relevant government agencies, and seek advice from a trusted professional
  • If your personal information has been published online without your consent — most platforms have a reporting process for this (Facebook, Google have content removal processes)
  • If you are experiencing online harassment or threats using your personal information — contact the Royal Papua New Guinea Constabulary (RPNGC)
  • For businesses that need a formal data privacy policy or need to handle customer data safely — consult a cybersecurity professional
