How to Identify Online Scams, Fake Links, and Phishing Messages

Audience: Everyone — home users, parents, business owners, youth
Difficulty: Beginner
Time: 15 minutes
Last reviewed: June 2026

1 Quick Summary

Online scams are a growing problem in Papua New Guinea, affecting individuals, families, and businesses. Scammers use Facebook, WhatsApp, SMS, and email to trick people into giving away money, passwords, or personal information.

This guide explains the most common scam types targeting PNG users and gives practical steps to identify and avoid fake messages, suspicious links, and social engineering attacks. You do not need any technical knowledge — the skills in this guide are about awareness and habit.

What you will learn: The 6 most common scam types in PNG, how to check if a message or link is genuine, what to do if you receive or respond to a scam, and how to report scams to protect others.

2 PNG Reality Check

PNG Reality Check

Scams targeting PNG users commonly include: fake job offers shared on Facebook or sent via SMS, fake bank messages requesting login details, impersonation of Digicel or bmobile account offers, marketplace scams (seller takes payment but does not deliver goods), fake government grant messages, and WhatsApp chain messages with false prize claims.

Scammers often use the names and logos of real organisations — BSP Bank, Kina Bank, Digicel, bmobile, BPNG (Bank of Papua New Guinea), the PNG Government, and even well-known local businesses — to appear legitimate. The use of a real logo or name does NOT mean the message is genuine.

Facebook Marketplace scams are particularly common in PNG. Buyers pay for goods — phones, vehicles, household items — and the seller either does not exist or disappears after payment. ASSUMED — based on regional pattern reports. Confirm with PNG law enforcement data

3 Safety Warning

Stop — If a Message Promises Money, Prizes, or Jobs in Exchange for Details or Fees

If a message promises money, prizes, a job, or government payments in exchange for personal details or an upfront fee — stop. Legitimate organisations do not ask you to pay money in order to receive money. This is the defining feature of almost every financial scam.

If you are unsure whether a message is real, call the organisation directly using a phone number from their official website — not a number given in the message itself. If you cannot find an official number, visit the organisation's physical office.

If you have already sent money or given personal details to a scammer, report it to your bank immediately and contact the Papua New Guinea Royal Constabulary. You are not alone and you are not to blame — these scams are sophisticated and deliberately target people who are not aware of them.

4 Common Scam Types in PNG

1. Phishing Messages

Phishing means sending fake messages that look like they come from a trusted organisation — your bank, Facebook, Digicel — to trick you into entering your login details on a fake website, or responding with your account information.

Example: An SMS says "Your BSP account has been suspended. Click here to verify: bsp-secure-login.com" — the link goes to a fake page that steals your username and password.

What makes it suspicious: Real banks do not send links asking you to log in via SMS. Real Facebook messages do not come from random phone numbers.

2. Fake Job Offers

Fake job offers promise high-paying work — often overseas, in hospitality, mining, construction, or domestic work — in exchange for personal details or an upfront fee for "processing", "visa", or "registration".

Example: A Facebook post advertises domestic work in Australia paying K5,000 per week. To apply, you must send K500 for "visa processing" to a mobile money account.

What makes it suspicious: Legitimate overseas employment agencies are registered in PNG. They do not ask for upfront payments via mobile money. Check any job offer against the official PNG National Narcotics Bureau or DBET (Department of Business, Trade and Employment) registered recruiters. 3. Marketplace and Online Selling Scams

Scammers post items for sale on Facebook Marketplace, Facebook groups, or in WhatsApp groups. The item may not exist, or the seller disappears after receiving payment.

Example: A listing shows a secondhand Toyota Hilux at a very low price. The seller asks for a deposit via mobile money before you can view the vehicle. After you pay, they stop responding.

What makes it suspicious: Prices significantly below market value, sellers who are unwilling to meet in person, sellers who only accept mobile money before the buyer sees the item, Facebook accounts that are new or have few friends or posts.

4. Account Recovery and Suspension Scams

Messages claim your account (Facebook, email, banking app) will be deleted or suspended unless you click a link or provide your login details. This creates urgency to make you act without thinking.

Example: A WhatsApp message says "Your Facebook account will be permanently deleted in 24 hours due to suspicious activity. Click here to verify your identity: [suspicious link]"

What makes it suspicious: Facebook and other platforms do not notify you via WhatsApp. Genuine account warnings appear inside the platform itself — in your notifications or via email from an official address (e.g. @facebookmail.com).

5. Prize and Lottery Scams

Messages claim you have won a cash prize, phone, vehicle, or airline ticket. To receive the prize, you must pay a "release fee", "customs fee", or provide your bank details.

Example: "Congratulations! You have been selected as a winner in the Digicel PNG National Prize Draw. To receive your K10,000 prize, pay a processing fee of K200 to [mobile number]."

What makes it suspicious: You cannot win a competition you did not enter. Legitimate prizes never require payment to release. Digicel and bmobile's official promotions are announced publicly — search for them on the company's official website or social media page.

6. Impersonation Scams

Scammers create fake social media accounts or use hacked accounts to impersonate a real person — a family member, employer, church leader, or friend — and ask for money urgently.

Example: A message from what appears to be your cousin's Facebook account says they have been in an accident and urgently need K500 transferred. The real account has been hacked.

What makes it suspicious: Unexpected requests for money, even from known contacts. If in doubt, call the person directly using a phone number you already have — not one provided in the message.

5 How to Spot a Fake Message — 5 Checks

Check 1

Verify the Sender

Look carefully at who sent the message. Ask yourself: is this a real phone number, email, or account that genuinely belongs to the organisation?

  • Official bank SMS messages come from registered short codes or consistent numbers — not random mobile numbers.
  • Official Facebook messages come from within the Facebook app — not from WhatsApp or SMS with Facebook's name.
  • Check the sender's email domain. "support@facebook.com" is real. "facebook-support@gmail.com" or "facebook@facebookmail-security.com" are fake.
  • On Facebook, click on a person's profile to check whether it is a real, established account with history and friends — or a new blank account.
Check 2

Check Any Link Before Clicking

Fake websites often use URLs that look similar to real ones, with small differences or extra words.

  • On a computer: hover your mouse over a link (without clicking) to see the real destination URL in the bottom of the browser.
  • On a phone: press and hold a link (long-press) to see where it actually leads before opening it.
  • Look for misspellings: "faceb00k.com", "bsp-bank-png.com", "digicel-png-prize.com" are all fake domains.
  • Real organisations use their own domain. Facebook uses facebook.com. BSP uses bsp.com.pg. If the URL uses a different domain with the company's name added, it is a scam.
  • Look for "https://" — but note that fake sites can also use https. A padlock icon does not automatically mean the site is safe — it only means the connection is encrypted. You still need to check the domain name.
Check 3

Look for Pressure Tactics

Scammers create urgency to stop you from thinking clearly. Legitimate organisations do not pressure you to act immediately or threaten consequences within hours.

Warning phrases that indicate a scam:

  • "Act now or your account will be deleted"
  • "You must respond within 24 hours"
  • "This offer expires today only"
  • "Do not tell anyone about this"
  • "You have been specially selected"
  • "Transfer the money immediately"
If a message makes you feel rushed, alarmed, or excited — slow down. That feeling is the goal of the scam. Take time to verify before acting.
Check 4

Verify Independently

If a message claims to be from your bank, ISP, or government agency, do not use the contact details in the message. Find the organisation's real contact information separately.

  • Call your bank using the number printed on the back of your bank card or the number on the bank's official website.
  • Visit the organisation's official website by typing the address yourself — do not click a link in the message.
  • Call the person who supposedly sent you a money request, using a phone number you already have from before — not a number given in the message.
Check 5

Never Pay Upfront to Receive Money

This is the single most reliable rule: if you have to pay money to receive money, it is a scam. No exceptions. This applies to:

  • Prize claims and lottery winnings
  • Overseas job placement fees
  • Government grants and payments
  • Inheritance or unclaimed funds
  • Business investments or partnerships from strangers

6 What to Do If You Received or Responded to a Scam

Step 1

Do Not Click Any Links or Reply Further

Stop the interaction immediately. Do not reply, do not click links, and do not provide any additional information.

Step 2

Block and Report the Sender

On Facebook: go to the scammer's profile or message → tap the three dots → Report → Scam or Fraud → Block. On WhatsApp: open the chat → tap the three dots → Report → Block. On SMS: use your phone's built-in block and report spam feature.

Reporting helps the platform identify and remove the scammer's account faster, protecting others.

Step 3

If You Clicked a Link — Change Your Password Immediately

If you clicked a suspicious link and entered any login details, change those account passwords immediately. Enable 2FA if you have not already (see the Two-Factor Authentication guide). Check for any activity on your account you did not do.

Step 4

If You Sent Money — Contact Your Bank Immediately

Call your bank using the official number on the back of your card or on the bank's official website. Do not use numbers from the scam message. Explain what happened. Banks may be able to reverse some transactions if contacted quickly. Mobile money transfers are harder to reverse — report them to Digicel, bmobile, or your mobile money provider's fraud line.

Step 5

Tell Others

Share what happened with family and friends so they can avoid the same scam. You do not need to be embarrassed — scammers are skilled at what they do. Warning others is one of the most useful things you can do after experiencing a scam attempt.

8 What If...

Situation What to Do
I am not sure if a message is a scam Do not act until you verify. Contact the organisation directly using official contact details. If in doubt, assume it is a scam and do not respond.
My friend or family member is about to respond to a scam Show them this guide. Encourage them to slow down and verify before paying or providing personal details. You are not being unhelpful — you may be preventing significant harm.
A scammer has my personal details (name, phone number, ID) Be alert for follow-up contact. Report to RPNGC if you suspect identity fraud. Monitor your bank accounts for unusual activity. Inform your bank that your details may have been compromised.
A scammer has access to my Facebook account Go to facebook.com/hacked immediately. Follow the account recovery steps. Change your email and Facebook password from a secure device. Enable 2FA once you regain access.
I paid a scammer via mobile money and cannot reverse it Contact Digicel or bmobile's fraud line. Contact RPNGC to file a report. Even if money cannot be recovered, a report helps authorities identify the scammer.
