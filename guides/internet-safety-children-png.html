Internet Safety for Children: Parent Setup Guide

Audience: Parents and guardians
Difficulty: Beginner
Time: 45 minutes
Last reviewed: June 2026

1 Quick Summary

This guide helps parents and guardians set up safer internet use for children. It covers parental controls on common devices, safe search settings, screen time management, family rules about internet use, and — most importantly — how to have honest, open conversations with your child about being safe online.

Technical controls are useful, but they are not enough on their own. Children who understand why online safety matters are better equipped than children who are simply blocked from the internet. This guide gives you both the technical steps and the conversation guidance.

What you will achieve: After completing this guide, SafeSearch will be enabled on shared devices, screen time limits will be in place, content restrictions will be set on the family device, your home Wi-Fi router will filter inappropriate content, and you will have had a practical safety conversation with your child.
Age note: Steps in this guide are most relevant for children aged 6–15. For teenagers aged 15–18, a conversation-based approach (see Step 1) becomes more important than technical blocking, which teenagers can often work around.

2 Before You Start

What you need

  • The device your child uses most — a phone, tablet, or computer
  • The device's PIN or password (you need to access Settings)
  • For Google Family Link: a Google account for yourself and one for your child
  • For iOS Screen Time: the family device, and an Apple ID if used
  • Access to your Wi-Fi router admin panel (see the Secure Your Wi-Fi Router guide for help with this)
  • About 45 minutes total

Start with the conversation — not the controls

Before making any technical changes, Step 1 below explains how to talk with your child about what you are doing and why. Children who understand online safety — rather than just experiencing restrictions — are far better protected, especially when they are online without parental supervision.

3 PNG Reality Check

PNG Reality Check

In PNG, children and young people often access the internet through shared family phones, home computers, school computers, and internet cafés. Many children are introduced to social media at a young age — sometimes through a parent's or older sibling's account.

Parents may not always be present when children use the internet, particularly when children use shared devices, school facilities, or access the internet at a friend's house. The technical controls in this guide protect children on devices and networks you control — but not everywhere they go online.

This is why the conversation in Step 1 is the most important part of this guide. A child who has been taught about online safety can apply that knowledge in places where technical controls do not exist. 4 Important Guidance for Parents

Age Limits and Account Safety

Children under 13 should not have their own social media accounts according to the Terms of Service of most platforms including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. If your child is using an account under an adult's name or age, this creates risks including exposure to adult content and unwanted contact from strangers.

If your child is under 13 and already using social media, consider using Google Family Link or Apple Screen Time to restrict app access, and have an honest conversation about moving them to a child-appropriate alternative (YouTube Kids, for example) rather than full YouTube.

Supervision versus prohibition: Children who are completely banned from the internet often find ways around restrictions and may be less likely to come to a parent when something goes wrong. Children who are taught about online safety — with reasonable, explained boundaries — are generally better equipped and more likely to ask for help when they need it.

5 Step-by-Step Instructions

Step 1

Have an Honest Conversation with Your Child

This is the most important step. Technology can be bypassed, but knowledge and trust cannot. Have this conversation before making any technical changes — explain what you are doing and why.

What to cover in the conversation

  • Explain your intention: Tell your child that you are setting up safety features, not punishing them. You are doing this because you care about their safety, the same way you have rules about road safety or talking to strangers.
  • Agree on screen time rules together: Children who are involved in setting rules are more likely to follow them. Ask your child what they think is a fair amount of screen time for school nights and weekends.
  • Teach what is never safe to share online: Full name, home address, school name, phone number, photos that show your location, and any personal information. Explain that online strangers — even people who seem friendly and young — cannot always be trusted.
  • Create a safety agreement: Tell your child to come to you if they see something online that upsets, confuses, or scares them. Make clear that they will not be in trouble for telling you — even if they were somewhere online they should not have been.
  • Explain that you will be checking: Let your child know that you will periodically check in on what they are accessing. This is not spying — it is the same care you would take with any other part of their life. Telling them in advance respects their trust.
A short, calm conversation is more effective than a long lecture. The goal is openness, not compliance. Return to this conversation regularly as your child gets older.
Step 2

Enable SafeSearch on Google

SafeSearch filters explicit content from Google Search results. It is not perfect — some content may still appear — but it significantly reduces accidental exposure to inappropriate material.

On a browser (all devices)

  1. Open a browser and go to google.com/preferences
  2. Under "SafeSearch filters", select Turn on SafeSearch.
  3. Scroll down and click Save.

Lock SafeSearch on Android (so children cannot change it)

  1. Sign in to your Google account on the device.
  2. Go to google.com/preferences → Turn on SafeSearch.
  3. To lock it (if you have a Google Workspace for Education account or Family Link): go to Google Family Link → Content restrictions → Google Search → turn on SafeSearch.

On YouTube

  1. Open YouTube and tap your profile picture → Settings → General → Restricted Mode → turn on.
  2. For younger children, consider using YouTube Kids instead of full YouTube. It has a dedicated children's content library and stronger filters.
Step 3

Set Up Parental Controls on Android (Google Family Link)

Google Family Link is a free app that allows parents to manage a child's Android device: set screen time limits, approve app downloads, see app activity, and restrict content.

Requirements

  • A Google account for yourself (the parent/guardian)
  • A Google account for your child (you create this through Family Link for children under 13)
  • Both the Family Link parent app (on your phone) and Family Link child app (on the child's device)

Setup steps

  1. On your phone: download Google Family Link for Parents from the Play Store.
  2. Open the app and tap "Create a Google Account for your child". Follow the prompts to create or link a child Google account.
  3. On your child's device: sign in with the child's Google account. The device will be supervised by your Family Link account.
  4. In the Family Link parent app, configure:
    • Daily limits — set a total daily screen time allowance
    • Bedtime — set hours when the device is locked automatically
    • App controls — require your approval before the child can download or install new apps
    • Content restrictions — set the minimum age rating for apps, games, and films that can be downloaded
    • Location — see where the device is (optional — discuss this with your child)
Google Family Link works while the child's device has internet access. If the device is offline, some restrictions may not apply. Review the Family Link settings periodically as your child grows and their needs change.
Step 4

Set Up Parental Controls on iPhone or iPad (Apple Screen Time)

Apple Screen Time is built into all iPhones and iPads running iOS 12 or later. It does not require downloading anything extra.

  1. On the child's iPhone or iPad, go to Settings → Screen Time.
  2. Tap Turn On Screen Time.
  3. Tap This is My Child's [device].
  4. Set a Screen Time Passcode — a 4-digit code only you know. This prevents your child from changing the settings. Do not use your child's birthday as the passcode.
  5. Configure:
    • Downtime — set hours when only specific apps (like Phone and Messages) are available. Useful for school hours and bedtime.
    • App Limits — set a daily time limit for specific app categories (e.g. Social Networking: 30 minutes per day).
    • Content & Privacy Restrictions:
      • Allowed Apps — turn off apps you do not want accessed (e.g. Safari, if you want to restrict all browsing)
      • Content Restrictions → Web Content → set to "Limit Adult Websites"
      • App Store Purchases → In-App Purchases → set to "Don't Allow"
      • Apps → set minimum app age rating (e.g. 9+ or 12+)
Older teenagers may know workarounds. Screen Time is most effective for younger children. For teenagers, combine it with open conversation (Step 1).
Step 5

Use a Family-Safe DNS on Your Wi-Fi Router

DNS (Domain Name System) is what your router uses to look up website addresses. By changing your router's DNS to a family-safe DNS server, you can block known adult content sites at the network level — affecting all devices in your home automatically.

This is particularly useful for devices you cannot directly control (friends' phones, gaming consoles, older devices).

Recommended family DNS options

  • Cloudflare Family (1.1.1.3) — blocks malware and adult content. Free. DNS addresses: Primary 1.1.1.3, Secondary 1.0.0.3
  • CleanBrowsing (Family Filter) — blocks adult content, proxies, and mixed content. Free. Primary 185.228.168.168, Secondary 185.228.169.168

How to change DNS on your router

  1. Log in to your router admin panel (see the Secure Your Wi-Fi Router guide for instructions).
  2. Find DNS Settings — usually under Internet, WAN, or Advanced settings.
  3. Change the Primary DNS to 1.1.1.3 (Cloudflare Family) and Secondary DNS to 1.0.0.3.
  4. Save settings and restart the router.
DNS-based filtering can be bypassed by a knowledgeable user who changes their device's DNS settings manually. It is one layer of protection — not a complete solution. Combine with device-level controls and conversation.
More detail: Changing router DNS is also covered in the Content Filtering at Home guide — see All Guides.
Step 6

Review What Your Child Is Accessing — Periodically

Parental controls and DNS filtering are not set-and-forget solutions. Children grow, their internet use changes, and new apps and platforms emerge. Regular check-ins are important.

  • Periodically check the device's browser history — do this openly rather than secretly, and let your child know it is a normal safety check, not a punishment.
  • Check the Family Link or Screen Time activity reports — these show which apps were used and for how long.
  • Have regular, informal conversations about what your child is watching, playing, and who they are talking to online. Make these feel like normal conversations, not interrogations.
  • Stay aware of new apps and platforms that become popular among young people. Search for them by name to understand what they are before your child uses them.
  • Revisit the conversation from Step 1 annually, or sooner if something concerning happens.

6 What Not to Do

  • Do not rely on blocking alone without conversation. Children who are blocked from content without understanding why often find workarounds, and are less likely to come to a parent when something goes wrong.
  • Do not take devices away as punishment without explanation. This creates a negative association between the parent and the device, rather than helping the child understand the issue.
  • Do not install hidden tracking or surveillance apps without the child's knowledge. Spyware-style monitoring damages trust and can harm the parent-child relationship. If you need to know what a child is doing online, be open about using parental controls rather than hidden surveillance.
  • Do not assume that school or internet café controls are sufficient. Apply home controls regardless of what exists elsewhere.
  • Do not shame a child for something they encountered online accidentally. Children who feel ashamed are less likely to tell a parent when something harmful happens. Respond to disclosures calmly.

7 When to Be Concerned

These signs may indicate that a child has had a concerning online experience or has made contact with someone who may pose a risk:

  • Child becomes secretive or upset when using their phone, or hides the screen when you approach
  • Child receives gifts, money, or packages from online contacts you do not know
  • Child mentions a new online-only friend who is an adult or much older teenager
  • Child seems withdrawn, upset, or anxious after being online
  • Child uses the internet late at night or at unusual times
  • Child minimises or closes screens when you enter the room
  • Child uses another person's device or asks to use the internet at a friend's place specifically to avoid home controls
If You Are Concerned About a Child's Safety

If you believe a child is in contact with someone who is grooming them, or if a child discloses that they have been sent inappropriate content or solicited online, contact the Royal Papua New Guinea Constabulary (RPNGC) and seek advice from a child protection professional.

Do not approach the suspected person yourself. Preserve any evidence (screenshots of messages) before blocking and reporting. Stay calm and supportive when the child discloses — their safety and trust in you matters more than any immediate reaction.

9 Troubleshooting

Problem What to Try
Google Family Link app is not available in the PNG Play Store Try installing it directly from the Google Play website on a computer (play.google.com). Alternatively, use the device's built-in Digital Wellbeing settings as a partial alternative. REQUIRES CONFIRMATION — verify Play Store availability of Family Link in PNG
Child has changed Screen Time passcode or disabled controls If using iOS: go to Settings → Screen Time → Change Screen Time Passcode. If the passcode is unknown, you may need to restore the device. Set a passcode that your child does not know — not their birthday.
Family Link controls not applying on the child's device Check that the child's device is connected to the internet and signed in to the supervised Google account. Family Link requires an internet connection to apply controls remotely.
Router DNS change did not take effect Restart the router after saving DNS settings. On devices that do not pick up the new DNS immediately, go to the device's Wi-Fi settings, forget the network, and reconnect.
Child is accessing internet through mobile data, bypassing router controls Mobile data bypasses Wi-Fi router DNS completely. Use Family Link or Screen Time to restrict data usage, or use a parental control app that works on mobile data as well (e.g. Circle, ESET Parental Controls).
Child used a VPN to bypass content filtering On Family Link, disable the ability to install new apps. On iOS Screen Time, turn off VPN installation under Content & Privacy Restrictions. Have a direct conversation about why content filtering exists.

10 When to Get Help

  • If you believe a child has been contacted by someone with harmful intent — contact RPNGC and a child protection professional immediately
  • If a child discloses that they have received inappropriate content, been asked to send photos, or has met someone online who has asked to meet in person — treat this as a child protection matter, not just a technology matter
  • If you are struggling to manage a teenager's internet use and feel out of your depth — consider family counselling or speaking to a school counsellor
  • For advanced network-level controls (e.g. business-grade content filtering for a school or community centre) — consult an ICT professional
